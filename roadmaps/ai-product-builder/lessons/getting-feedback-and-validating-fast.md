## Speed is the whole point of a fast prototype

The entire value of building a prototype quickly with AI tools is that it buys you a cheap way to find out whether an idea holds up before you've invested real engineering time in it. That value is wasted if the prototype then sits on a laptop instead of going in front of real people. The point of this stage isn't to make something impressive — it's to generate a reaction you can learn from, as fast as possible, from someone whose opinion actually predicts whether the idea works. This lesson's video follows that exact loop in a specific, concrete setting: a sales team showing prototypes built from meeting notes directly to prospective customers. The context is specific, but the underlying loop it demonstrates — build fast, show a real person, listen, revise — is the same one that applies to validating any product idea, and it's worth watching for how directly and quickly that loop gets closed in practice.

## Who you show it to matters more than how polished it is

A prototype shown to someone who has no real stake in the problem — a supportive friend, a coworker being nice — will almost always get a positive reaction, because being encouraging costs that person nothing and they have no skin in whether the thing actually works. That reaction tells you about their social instincts, not about your idea. The people worth showing a prototype to are the ones who actually have the problem it claims to solve: people who'd be frustrated enough by the current state of things to have tried something else already, or who are currently paying for or working around a worse solution. Their reaction carries information precisely because they have something real to react against.

## Ask about behavior, not intention

The least useful question to ask after a demo is some version of "would you use this?" Almost everyone says yes to that question, because agreeing is easy and free, and people are generally bad at predicting their own future behavior even with good intentions. A far more reliable line of questioning asks about the past and present: what do you currently do instead? What have you tried that didn't work? What would you have had to do five minutes ago, before seeing this, to solve the same problem? Answers grounded in actual past behavior reveal real constraints, workarounds, and willingness to pay that hypothetical enthusiasm never does.

## Telling signal from politeness

Positive words in the room are the weakest evidence you'll get, because they're often just social lubrication. What separates genuine validation from polite interest is what happens without you asking for it: does the person bring it up again later unprompted, ask when they can use it for real, or show it to someone else on their own initiative? Those actions cost the person something — time, social capital, a bit of effort — in a way that a compliment during the demo does not, which is exactly why they're worth so much more as signal.

## The loop generalizes beyond any one audience

Strip away the specific setting of sales demos and customer meetings, and what's left is a general validation loop that applies anywhere you're trying to figure out if an idea is worth building further: get something real in front of a real person fast, ask about their actual situation rather than their hypothetical opinion, and pay attention to what they do next rather than what they say in the moment. Whether the "customer" in that loop is an external buyer, an internal stakeholder, or a friend with the actual problem you're solving, the mechanics of getting an honest read are the same, and they're cheapest to run while all you've built is a prototype.
